# Fundamentals of Electrical Engineering: Series RLC Circuits & Transient Response Analysis

---

## 1. High-Level Overview & Basic Concepts

### 1.1 What is a Series RLC Circuit?
A **Series RLC Circuit** consists of three basic electrical components connected in series with a voltage source ($V_S$):
* **Resistor ($R$):** Dissipates energy in the form of heat (measured in Ohms, $\Omega$).
* **Inductor ($L$):** Stores energy in a magnetic field (measured in Henries, $\text{H}$).
* **Capacitor ($C$):** Stores energy in an electric field (measured in Farads, $\text{F}$).

Because all elements are in series, the same instantaneous current $i(t)$ flows through every element, while the total supply voltage $V_S$ splits across the components as $v_R(t)$, $v_L(t)$, and $v_C(t)$.

---

### 1.2 Physical Analogy
Think of a Series RLC circuit as a physical **mass-spring-damper system** (like a car suspension):
* **Capacitor ($C$):** Behaves like a **spring** (stores mechanical potential energy).
* **Inductor ($L$):** Behaves like a **mass/inertia** (resists changes in motion/velocity).
* **Resistor ($R$):** Behaves like a **shock absorber/friction** (dissipates energy to stop oscillations).

---

### 1.3 Key Terminology
* **Transient Response:** The temporary, time-dependent behavior of the circuit immediately after a switch is closed or opened, before it settles into a steady state.
* **Natural Frequency ($\omega_0$):** The rate at which the system naturally wants to oscillate when energy is released, given by:
  $$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \quad \text{(rad/s)}$$
* **Damping Rate ($\alpha$):** The rate at which energy is dissipated by the resistor, given by:
  $$\alpha = \frac{R}{2L} \quad \text{(1/s)}$$
* **Damped Frequency ($\omega_d$):** The actual frequency at which the circuit oscillates in an underdamped condition:
  $$\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2}$$
* **BIBO Stability:** **Bounded Input, Bounded Output**. A system is stable if any finite input produces a finite (non-infinite) output.

## 2. Derivation of the Governing Differential Equation

### 2.1 Circuit Diagram & KVL Setup

![Diagram: Series RLC Circuit](./images/series_rlc_circuit.png)
*Description of Visual Layout:* A single-loop schematic featuring a DC voltage source $V_S$ connected in series with a knife switch, a resistor $R$, an inductor $L$, and a capacitor $C$. Polarities for component voltages $v_R$, $v_L$, and $v_C$ are marked, and current $i$ flows clockwise through the loop.

---

### 2.2 Mathematical Derivation
Applying Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL) around the loop after closing the switch at $t = 0$:

$$V_S = v_R(t) + v_L(t) + v_C(t) \quad \text{--- (1)}$$

Using the fundamental terminal relationships of electrical components:
1. Resistor voltage: $v_R(t) = i(t) R$
2. Inductor voltage: $v_L(t) = L \frac{di(t)}{dt}$
3. Capacitor current relation: $i(t) = C \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt} \quad \text{--- (2)}$

Substitute equation (2) into the voltage expressions for $v_R$ and $v_L$:
* $v_R(t) = R C \frac{dv_C}{dt}$
* $v_L(t) = L \frac{d}{dt}\left(C \frac{dv_C}{dt}\right) = LC \frac{d^2 v_C}{dt^2}$

Substitute these into equation (1):

$$V_S = R C \frac{dv_C}{dt} + LC \frac{d^2 v_C}{dt^2} + v_C$$

Divide the entire equation by $LC$ to obtain the standard second-order linear differential equation:

$$\frac{d^2 v_C}{dt^2} + \frac{R}{L} \frac{dv_C}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC} v_C = \frac{1}{LC} V_S \quad \text{--- (3)}$$

---

## 3. General Solution Structure

The complete solution to a non-homogeneous linear second-order differential equation consists of two parts:

$$v_C(t) = v_{c.f.}(t) + v_{p.i.}$$

* **Complementary Function ($v_{c.f.}$):** Represents the natural transient response of the system as it decays over time.
* **Particular Integral ($v_{p.i.}$):** Represents the forced response (steady-state value). For a constant DC input $V_S$, the particular integral is:
  $$v_{p.i.} = V_S$$

---

### 3.1 Characteristic Equation
To find $v_{c.f.}(t)$, assume a solution of the form $v_C(t) = A e^{st}$. Substituting this trial solution yields the **characteristic equation**:

$$s^2 + \frac{R}{L} s + \frac{1}{LC} = 0$$

Rewriting using damping rate $\alpha = \frac{R}{2L}$ and natural frequency $\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$:

$$s^2 + 2\alpha s + \omega_0^2 = 0$$

Using the quadratic formula, the roots $s_1$ and $s_2$ are:

$$s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2}$$

---

## 4. Analysis of Response Types (Decided by Resistance $R$)

The physical behavior of the circuit depends entirely on the discriminant $(\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2)$, which is governed by the value of resistance $R$.

| Response Type | Condition | Roots $s_1, s_2$ | Voltage Response Equation $v_C(t)$ | System Stability |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Critically Damped** | $\alpha = \omega_0$ | Real and equal ($s_1 = s_2 = -\alpha$) | $v_C(t) = e^{-\alpha t}(A_1 t + A_2) + V_S$ | Stable (Fastest settling without overshoot) |
| **Overdamped** | $\alpha > \omega_0$ | Real and distinct | $v_C(t) = (A_1 e^{-\alpha_1 t} + A_2 e^{-\alpha_2 t}) + V_S$ | Stable (Sluggish response, no oscillation) |
| **Underdamped** | $\alpha < \omega_0$ | Complex conjugate ($s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm j\omega_d$) | $v_C(t) = e^{-\alpha t}\left(A_1 \cos(\omega_d t) + A_2 \sin(\omega_d t)\right) + V_S$ | Stable (Oscillates while decaying to steady state) |
| **Undamped (Oscillator)** | $R = 0 \implies \alpha = 0$ | Purely imaginary ($s_{1,2} = \pm j\omega_0$) | $v_C(t) = A_1 \cos(\omega_0 t) + A_2 \sin(\omega_0 t) + V_S$ | Marginally Stable (Sustained endless oscillations) |
| **Unstable** | $R < 0 \implies \alpha < 0$ | Real parts positive | $v_C(t) \propto e^{+\alpha t} \dots$ | Unstable (Uncontrolled exponential growth) |

---

### 4.1 Detailed Breakdown of Each Case

#### Case 1: Critically Damped Response ($\alpha = \omega_0$)
* **Resistance Condition:** $R = 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$
* **Behavior:** The system returns to steady state as quickly as possible without oscillating or overshooting. Ideal for control systems where overshoot must be avoided.

#### Case 2: Overdamped Response ($\alpha > \omega_0$)
* **Resistance Condition:** $R > 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$
* **Behavior:** The energy is dissipated quickly by the high resistance. The voltage slowly approaches $V_S$ without any oscillations.

#### Case 3: Underdamped Response ($\alpha < \omega_0$)
* **Resistance Condition:** $R < 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$
* **Behavior:** The stored energy bounces back and forth between the inductor and capacitor before being fully dissipated by $R$. This results in decaying sinusoidal oscillations at frequency $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2}$.

---

### 4.2 Special Cases: Pure Oscillation & Instability

#### Case 4: Undamped Oscillator ($R = 0$)

![Diagram: Undamped LC Oscillator Circuit and Waveforms](./images/undamped_lc_circuit.png)
*Description of Visual Layout:* Left: Circuit diagram showing a DC source $V_S$, switch, Inductor $L$, and Capacitor $C$ without any resistor ($R=0$). Right: Time-domain plots showing constant-amplitude sinusoidal oscillations for capacitor voltage $v_C(t)$, inductor voltage $v_L(t)$, and capacitor current $i(t)$.

* **Characteristic Equation:** $s^2 + \frac{1}{LC} = 0 \implies s = \pm j\omega_0$
* **Physical Significance:** Energy continually sloshes back and forth between the magnetic field of $L$ and the electric field of $C$ with zero thermal loss. The circuit behaves as a pure oscillator producing continuous sine waves.

#### Case 5: Negative Resistance / Unstable System ($R < 0$)

![Diagram: Negative Resistance Waveform Growth](./images/negative_resistance_growth.png)
*Description of Visual Layout:* Graph displaying capacitor voltage versus time. After $t = 0.2\text{ ms}$, the waveform exhibits sinusoidal oscillations whose peak-to-peak amplitude exponentially grows larger over time rather than decaying.

* **Characteristic Equation:** $s^2 + \left(-\frac{R}{L}\right)s + \frac{1}{LC} = 0$
* **Physical Significance:** A negative real part of $\alpha$ translates mathematically to an expanding exponential factor ($e^{+\alpha t}$). The system continuously injects energy into the circuit, causing uncontrolled growing oscillations that lead to instability.

## 5. Step-by-Step Worked Examples

### Problem 1: Initial/Final State & Critical Resistance Calculation

**Problem Statement:**
An RLC series circuit is initially relaxed (all initial voltages and currents are zero). At $t = 0\text{ s}$, a switch connects a DC voltage source $V_S = 5\text{ V}$. The circuit parameters are $L = 1\text{ mH}$ ($1 \times 10^{-3}\text{ H}$) and $C = 1\text{ }\mu\text{F}$ ($1 \times 10^{-6}\text{ F}$).

![Diagram: Illustration Problem Circuit Schema](./images/illustration1_circuit.png)
*Description of Visual Layout:* Series RLC circuit containing a $5\text{ V}$ DC supply, switch closed at $t=0$, variable resistor $R$, $1\text{ mH}$ inductor, and $1\mu\text{F}$ capacitor.

**Evaluate:**
1. $v_C(0^+)$ and $v_C(\infty)$
2. $i_L(0^+)$ and $i_L(\infty)$
3. The value of $R$ required for a **critically damped response**.

---

#### Solution:

**Part (i): Finding Capacitor Voltages $v_C(0^+)$ and $v_C(\infty)$**

1. **At $t = 0^+$ (Immediately after switch closure):**
   * Fundamental property of a capacitor: Voltage across a capacitor **cannot change instantaneously** because $i_C = C \frac{dv_C}{dt}$.
   * Since the circuit was initially relaxed ($v_C(0^-) = 0\text{ V}$):
     $$v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-) = 0\text{ V}$$

2. **At $t = \infty$ (DC Steady State):**
   * In a DC steady-state condition, the capacitor acts as an **open circuit** ($\frac{dv_C}{dt} = 0 \implies i = 0$).
   * Zero current means zero voltage drop across $R$ and $L$.
   * Therefore, the entire supply voltage appears across the capacitor:
     $$v_C(\infty) = V_S = 5\text{ V}$$

---

**Part (ii): Finding Inductor Currents $i_L(0^+)$ and $i_L(\infty)$**

1. **At $t = 0^+$ (Immediately after switch closure):**
   * Fundamental property of an inductor: Current through an inductor **cannot change instantaneously** because $v_L = L \frac{di}{dt}$.
   * Since the circuit was relaxed ($i_L(0^-) = 0\text{ A}$):
     $$i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-) = 0\text{ A}$$

2. **At $t = \infty$ (DC Steady State):**
   * As established in Part (i), the fully charged capacitor acts as an open circuit, blocking current flow through the series path.
   * Therefore:
     $$i_L(\infty) = 0\text{ A}$$

---

**Part (iii): Resistance $R$ for Critical Damping**

For a critically damped system, the damping rate $\alpha$ must equal the natural frequency $\omega_0$:

$$\alpha = \omega_0$$

Substitute definitions:

$$\frac{R}{2L} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$

Solve for $R$:

$$R = 2 \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$$

Substitute the given numerical values ($L = 1 \times 10^{-3}\text{ H}$, $C = 1 \times 10^{-6}\text{ F}$):

$$R = 2 \sqrt{\frac{1 \times 10^{-3}}{1 \times 10^{-6}}} = 2 \sqrt{1000} = 2 \times 31.6227 = 63.25\text{ }\Omega$$

**Final Calculated Values:**
* $v_C(0^+) = 0\text{ V}$
* $v_C(\infty) = 5\text{ V}$
* $i_L(0^+) = 0\text{ A}$
* $i_L(\infty) = 0\text{ A}$
* $R_{\text{critical}} = 63.25\text{ }\Omega$

---

## 6. Key Takeaways & Exam Summary Checklist

* **Second-Order System:** Transient behavior in a series RLC circuit is modeled by a 2nd order differential equation:
  $$\frac{d^2 v_C}{dt^2} + 2\alpha \frac{dv_C}{dt} + \omega_0^2 v_C = \omega_0^2 V_S$$
* **Role of Resistance ($R$):** $R$ determines energy dissipation and governs system classification:
  * $R > 2\sqrt{L/C} \implies \text{Overdamped}$ (Slow, non-oscillatory)
  * $R = 2\sqrt{L/C} \implies \text{Critically Damped}$ (Fastest non-oscillatory convergence)
  * $R < 2\sqrt{L/C} \implies \text{Underdamped}$ (Decaying oscillation)
  * $R = 0 \implies \text{Undamped Oscillator}$ (Continuous oscillation at $\omega_0$)
  * $R < 0 \implies \text{Unstable System}$ (Exponentially growing amplitude)
* **Continuity Rules for Initial Conditions:**
  * Capacitor Voltage cannot jump instantaneously: $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$
  * Inductor Current cannot jump instantaneously: $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$
