## 1. High-Level Overview & Intuitive Foundations

### What is Capacitance?
In electrical engineering, a **capacitor** is a passive electrical component designed to store energy in an **electric field**. Unlike a battery, which generates electrical energy through internal chemical reactions, a capacitor merely holds electrical charge physically on conductive plates separated by an insulating medium (dielectric).

- **Core Intuition:** Imagine a capacitor as an elastic water storage balloon in a pipe system. When water (current) flows in, the balloon expands, building back-pressure (voltage). The balloon resists rapid changes in water level.
- **Fundamental Law:** Capacitance ($C$) is the fundamental property of a capacitor that **opposes any sudden rate of change of voltage** across its terminals.

```markdown

+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                               KEY CONCEPT                                   |
| Voltage across a capacitor CANNOT change instantaneously.                   |
| To change voltage instantly would require an infinite amount of current,    |
| which is physically impossible in real circuits: $i_c = C \frac{dv_c}{dt}$. |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+

```

### Governing Mathematical Equations

1. **Stored Energy:**
   The energy $E$ stored within the electrostatic field of a capacitor is directly proportional to its capacitance and the square of the voltage across it:
   $$E = \frac{1}{2} C V^2 \quad \text{[Joules, J]}$$

2. **Current-Voltage Relationship:**
   Current through a capacitor depends on how quickly the voltage across it changes over time (the derivative of voltage with respect to time):
   $$i_c(t) = C \frac{d v_c(t)}{d t} \quad \text{[Amperes, A]}$$

3. **Voltage as an Integral of Current:**
   By integrating the current equation, the instantaneous voltage across the capacitor is accumulated charges over time:
   $$v_c(t) = \frac{1}{C} \int i_c(t) \, dt \quad \text{[Volts, V]}$$

## 2. Transient Behavior in RC Series Circuits

### What is a "Transient" State?
When a switch changes position in a circuit containing capacitors or inductors, the circuit undergoes a transition period before settling into a steady condition. 

- **Transient State:** The temporary response of a circuit during the transition phase right after a disturbance (e.g., closing/opening a switch).
- **Steady State:** The long-term behavior of the circuit after all voltages and currents have settled into fixed or periodic patterns ($t \to \infty$).

---

### Mathematical Derivation of Circuit Differential Equation

Consider a series $RC$ circuit connected to a constant DC voltage source $V$ via a switch that closes at time $t = 0$.

![Diagram: Series RC Circuit with DC Voltage Source](./images/rc_series_circuit.png)
*Visual Layout Description:* A simple single-mesh loop containing a DC voltage source $V$ on the left branch, connected in series with a single-pole single-throw switch that closes at $t=0$, followed by a resistor $R$ on the top branch, and a capacitor $C$ on the right branch. The current $i_C(t)$ flows clockwise around the loop, and voltage polarity across $C$ is marked positive at the top terminal and negative at the bottom terminal as $v_C(t)$.

#### Step 1: Apply Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)
Applying KVL around the loop for $t \ge 0$:
$$V = v_R(t) + v_c(t)$$

Using Ohm's Law for the resistor, $v_R(t) = R \cdot i_c(t)$:
$$V = R i_c(t) + v_c(t)$$

#### Step 2: Substitute $i_c(t)$ into KVL
Substitute $i_c(t) = C \frac{d v_c(t)}{d t}$:
$$V = R C \frac{d v_c(t)}{d t} + v_c(t)$$

This is a first-order linear non-homogeneous differential equation.

#### Step 3: Solve the First-Order Differential Equation
The total solution $v_c(t)$ is composed of two parts:
$$v_c(t) = v_{c.f.}(t) + v_{p.i.}(t)$$

- **Complementary Function ($v_{c.f.}$):** Represents the **Transient Response** (natural response).
  $$v_{c.f.}(t) = K_1 e^{-t / RC}$$
- **Particular Integral ($v_{p.i.}$):** Represents the **Steady-State Response** (forced response).
  $$v_{p.i.}(t) = V$$

Combining both terms gives the general expression:
$$v_c(t) = K_1 e^{-t / RC} + V$$

#### Step 4: Apply Initial Boundary Conditions
Assuming the capacitor is completely uncharged before the switch closes at $t = 0^-$:
$$v_c(0^+) = v_c(0^-) = 0$$

Substitute $t = 0$ into the general equation:
$$0 = K_1 e^0 + V \implies K_1 = -V$$

#### Step 5: Final Expressions for Voltage and Current

1. **Capacitor Charging Voltage Equation:**
   $$v_c(t) = V \left(1 - e^{-t / RC}\right)$$

2. **Charging Current Equation:**
   $$i_c(t) = C \frac{d v_c}{d t} = C \cdot V \left(0 - \left(-\frac{1}{RC}\right)e^{-t / RC}\right)$$
   $$i_c(t) = \frac{V}{R} e^{-t / RC}$$

---

### The Time Constant ($\tau$)

The quantity $\tau = RC$ is defined as the **Time Constant** of the circuit (measured in seconds).

$$\tau = R \times C \quad \text{[Seconds, s]}$$

- **Physical Meaning:** $\tau$ dictates how rapidly the circuit responds.
- At $t = \tau$:
  $$v_c(\tau) = V \left(1 - e^{-1}\right) \approx V(1 - 0.368) = 0.632 V$$
  The time constant $\tau$ is the exact time required for the capacitor voltage to reach approximately **63.2%** of its maximum steady-state voltage.
- **Practical Rule of Thumb:** A circuit is considered to have reached steady state after **$5\tau$** (at $t = 5\tau$, $v_c \approx 99.3\%$ of $V$).

![Diagram: RC Charging Curve Graph](./images/rc_charging_graph.png)
*Visual Layout Description:* A 2D Cartesian plot showing time $t$ on the horizontal axis and capacitor voltage $v_c(t)$ on the vertical axis. The curve starts at the origin $(0,0)$ and curves upward smoothly, asymptotically approaching the maximum steady-state DC voltage $V=10\text{ V}$. A dashed tangent line from the origin reaches $V=10\text{ V}$ at time $t = \tau = 0.01\text{ s}$, illustrating the initial rate of rise.

## 3. Step-by-Step Worked Examples & Problems

### Problem 1: Full RC Circuit Parameter Analysis

**Problem Statement:**
A capacitor of $50\,\mu\text{F}$ in series with a $1\,\text{k}\Omega$ resistor is connected across a $100\,\text{V}$ DC supply. Calculate:
1. Time constant ($\tau$) of the charging circuit
2. Initial charging current ($I_0$)
3. Equation of current as a function of time ($i(t)$)
4. Value of current after $0.05\,\text{s}$
5. Charging current after $0.06\,\text{s}$
6. Equation of charge as a function of time ($q(t)$)
7. Voltage across the resistor after $0.06\,\text{s}$

---

#### Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

**Given Data:**
- $C = 50\,\mu\text{F} = 50 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{F}$
- $R = 1\,\text{k}\Omega = 1000\,\Omega$
- $V = 100\,\text{V}$

---

#### Part 1: Calculate Time Constant ($\tau$)
$$\tau = R \cdot C = (1000\,\Omega) \times (50 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{F}) = 0.05\,\text{s}$$

---

#### Part 2: Calculate Initial Charging Current ($I_0$)
At $t = 0$, $v_c(0) = 0\,\text{V}$. The entire supply voltage drops across $R$:
$$I_0 = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{100\,\text{V}}{1000\,\Omega} = 0.1\,\text{A} = 100\,\text{mA}$$

---

#### Part 3: Derive Equation of Current as a Function of Time $i(t)$
Using $i(t) = \frac{V}{R} e^{-t / \tau}$:
$$i(t) = 0.1 e^{-t / 0.05} = 0.1 e^{-20t} \quad \text{[A]}$$

---

#### Part 4: Calculate Current at $t = 0.05\,\text{s}$
Note that $t = 0.05\,\text{s} = 1\tau$:
$$i(0.05) = 0.1 \cdot e^{-0.05 / 0.05} = 0.1 \cdot e^{-1} = 0.1 \times 0.367879 = 0.03679\,\text{A} = 36.79\,\text{mA}$$

---

#### Part 5: Calculate Current at $t = 0.06\,\text{s}$
$$i(0.06) = 0.1 \cdot e^{-0.06 / 0.05} = 0.1 \cdot e^{-1.2} = 0.1 \times 0.30119 = 0.03012\,\text{A} = 30.12\,\text{mA}$$

---

#### Part 6: Derive Equation of Charge as a Function of Time $q(t)$
Since $q(t) = C \cdot v_c(t)$ and $v_c(t) = V(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$:
$$q(t) = C V (1 - e^{-t/\tau})$$
$$Q_{\max} = C V = (50 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{F}) \times (100\,\text{V}) = 5 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{C} = 5\,\text{mC}$$
$$q(t) = 5 \times 10^{-3} \left(1 - e^{-20t}\right) \quad \text{[C]}$$

---

#### Part 7: Calculate Voltage Across Resistor at $t = 0.06\,\text{s}$
Using Ohm's Law $v_R(t) = i(t) \cdot R$:
$$v_R(0.06) = (0.03012\,\text{A}) \times (1000\,\Omega) = 30.12\,\text{V}$$

*(Alternatively: $v_c(0.06) = 100(1 - e^{-1.2}) = 69.88\,\text{V} \implies v_R = 100 - 69.88 = 30.12\,\text{V}$)*

### Problem 2: Current-Driven RC Circuit & Target Voltage Time Computation

**Problem Statement:**
In the circuit shown below, switch $S$ is closed to position $1$ at time $t = 0$. How long does it take for the capacitor to attain $70\%$ of its final voltage? Assume the capacitor is initially uncharged. Find the time constant ($\tau$) of the circuit after the switch is closed.

![Diagram: Current Source Driven RC Circuit Switch Diagram](./images/rc_current_source_circuit.png)
*Visual Layout Description:* A multi-mesh circuit fed by a $1\text{ A}$ ideal current source on the far left branch. A single-pole switch $S$ at node $1$ closes at $t=0$. Branch 1 contains a $200\ \Omega$ vertical resistor connected directly across the node after the switch. To the right, a horizontal $100\ \Omega$ resistor connects to node $a$. From node $a$ to bottom node $b$, a $0.01\text{ F}$ capacitor is connected in parallel with a vertical $100\ \Omega$ resistor.

---

#### Step-by-Step Solution

#### Step 1: Simplify Circuit using Thévenin’s Equivalent as Seen by Capacitor
To simplify the network connected to capacitor $C = 0.01\,\text{F}$, replace everything to the left of terminals $a$-$b$ with its Thévenin Equivalent ($V_{th}$ and $R_{th}$).

1. **Find Open-Circuit Voltage ($V_{th}$):**
   Disconnect the capacitor at terminals $a$-$b$.
   The $1\,\text{A}$ current source flows through the parallel branch of the $200\,\Omega$ resistor and the branch containing $(100\,\Omega + 100\,\Omega)$:
   - Branch 1 Resistance: $200\,\Omega$
   - Branch 2 Resistance: $100\,\Omega + 100\,\Omega = 200\,\Omega$

   By current division, current through the right branch:
   $$I_{right} = 1\,\text{A} \times \frac{200}{200 + 200} = 0.5\,\text{A}$$

   The open-circuit voltage across terminals $a$-$b$ (across the rightmost $100\,\Omega$ resistor):
   $$V_{th} = I_{right} \times 100\,\Omega = 0.5\,\text{A} \times 100\,\Omega = 50\,\text{V}$$

2. **Find Thévenin Resistance ($R_{th}$):**
   Deactivate the independent current source (open-circuit it). Looking back into terminals $a$-$b$:
   - The $200\,\Omega$ resistor is in series with the top $100\,\Omega$ resistor $\implies 200 + 100 = 300\,\Omega$.
   - This $300\,\Omega$ combination is in parallel with the rightmost $100\,\Omega$ resistor.

   $$R_{th} = (200 + 100) \parallel 100 = 300 \parallel 100 = \frac{300 \times 100}{300 + 100} = \frac{30000}{400} = 75\,\Omega$$

---

#### Step 2: Calculate Equivalent Circuit Time Constant ($\tau$)
Using Thévenin equivalent parameters:
$$\tau = R_{th} \cdot C = 75\,\Omega \times 0.01\,\text{F} = 0.75\,\text{s}$$

---

#### Step 3: Compute Time to Reach 70% of Final Voltage
The target voltage is $v_c(t) = 0.70 \cdot V_{th}$.

Using the standard charging equation:
$$v_c(t) = V_{th} \left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$
$$0.70 \cdot V_{th} = V_{th} \left(1 - e^{-t/0.75}\right)$$
$$0.70 = 1 - e^{-t/0.75}$$
$$e^{-t/0.75} = 0.30$$

Taking the natural logarithm ($\ln$) on both sides:
$$-\frac{t}{0.75} = \ln(0.30)$$
$$-\frac{t}{0.75} = -1.20397$$
$$t = 1.20397 \times 0.75 \approx 0.903\,\text{s}$$

### Problem 3: Two-Position Switch Transition Analysis

**Problem Statement:**
In the circuit shown below, the switch has been in **position A** for a long time. At $t = 0$, the switch moves to **position B**.
1. Determine $v(t)$ for $t > 0$.
2. Calculate its value at $t = 1\,\text{s}$ and $t = 4\,\text{s}$.

![Diagram: Two Position Switch RC Circuit Analysis](./images/rc_two_position_switch.png)
*Visual Layout Description:* A multi-mesh circuit with two sub-circuits selectable via a single-pole double-throw switch. Left sub-circuit (Position A) contains a $24\text{ V}$ source in series with a $3\text{ k}\Omega$ resistor, connected in parallel across a $5\text{ k}\Omega$ resistor. Position B connects to a right sub-circuit containing a $4\text{ k}\Omega$ resistor in series with a $30\text{ V}$ source. A $0.5\text{ mF}$ capacitor is connected between the central switch wiper and ground.

---

#### Step-by-Step Solution

#### Phase 1: Determine Initial Condition at $t = 0^-$ (Switch in Position A)
Before $t = 0$, the switch was at position A for a "long time" ($t \to \infty$). Thus, the capacitor acts as an **open circuit** (fully charged).

Calculate the voltage across the $5\,\text{k}\Omega$ resistor using the voltage divider rule:
$$v_c(0^-) = 24\,\text{V} \times \frac{5\,\text{k}\Omega}{3\,\text{k}\Omega + 5\,\text{k}\Omega} = 24 \times \frac{5}{8} = 15\,\text{V}$$

Since capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously:
$$v(0^+) = v(0^-) = 15\,\text{V}$$

---

#### Phase 2: Determine Final Steady-State Condition as $t \to \infty$ (Switch in Position B)
At $t = 0$, the switch flips to position B. The left circuit is disconnected. The capacitor is now connected to the right branch containing $R = 4\,\text{k}\Omega$ and source $V = 30\,\text{V}$.

After a long time ($t \to \infty$), the capacitor charges to the new DC source voltage:
$$v(\infty) = 30\,\text{V}$$

---

#### Phase 3: Find Circuit Time Constant $\tau$ for $t > 0$
Looking from the capacitor terminals into Position B:
$$R_{eq} = 4\,\text{k}\Omega = 4000\,\Omega$$
$$C = 0.5\,\text{mF} = 0.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{F} = 500\,\mu\text{F}$$
$$\tau = R_{eq} \cdot C = 4000 \times (0.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.0\,\text{s}$$

---

#### Phase 4: Construct General Transient Equation for $v(t)$
The general expression for any first-order transient response is:
$$v(t) = v(\infty) + [v(0^+) - v(\infty)] e^{-t/\tau}$$

Substitute the values:
- $v(0^+) = 15\,\text{V}$
- $v(\infty) = 30\,\text{V}$
- $\tau = 2.0\,\text{s}$

$$v(t) = 30 + (15 - 30) e^{-t/2.0}$$
$$v(t) = 30 - 15 e^{-0.5t} \quad \text{[V]} \quad \text{for } t \ge 0$$

---

#### Phase 5: Calculate Voltage at Specified Time Instants

1. **At $t = 1\,\text{s}$:**
   $$v(1) = 30 - 15 e^{-0.5(1)} = 30 - 15 e^{-0.5} = 30 - 15(0.60653) = 30 - 9.098 = 20.902\,\text{V}$$

2. **At $t = 4\,\text{s}$:**
   $$v(4) = 30 - 15 e^{-0.5(4)} = 30 - 15 e^{-2.0} = 30 - 15(0.135335) = 30 - 2.030 = 27.970\,\text{V}$$
